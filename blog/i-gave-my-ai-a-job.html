I Don't Chat With AI. I Gave Mine a Job.

Sep 2026 · DaRoy Perkins

Most people use AI like a search bar. One question in, one answer out — then it forgets you exist. That's leaving 90% of the value on the table.

I run a 1.4 MW commercial solar jobsite. My days start at 5 AM. I don't have time to babysit a chatbot. So I did what I do on every job: I wrote it a job description.

The shift

Somewhere along the way I stopped asking my AI questions and started handing it standing responsibilities — jobs it does every day without being asked. That one change turned it from a chatbot into an operator.

Here are the five jobs it holds.

Job 1: The 6 AM brief

Every morning at 6 AM I get a brief: jobsite weather, what's on my calendar, and one thing worth thinking about. I haven't opened a weather app in months. I wake up already oriented — no scrolling, no assembling my day from five different apps.

Job 2: Money watchdog

It watches my spending across every account, flags subscriptions creeping up, and killed the ones I didn't need. Last week it ran a nationwide unclaimed-money sweep and found funds in my name held by Maryland. Real dollars — not productivity theater.

"That's the whole game — context compounds. Every interaction makes the next one sharper."

Job 3: Research department

Fishing trip? It checks tides, regulations, and slot limits before I pick a spot. Buying a laptop? It vets every option against what I actually need. Evaluating new tech for my side project? It reads the docs and hands me a verdict. I make the call — it does the homework.

Job 4: Memory

It remembers my crew members' names, my cost codes, my schedule, how I like my emails written, what I've already decided. I never brief it twice. That's the whole game — context compounds. Every interaction makes the next one sharper.

Job 5: The closer

Reminders that actually fire. Follow-ups that close loops. It doesn't just answer — it finishes things and tells me when they're done. The number of open loops in my life has collapsed.

Set yours up in 3 steps

  1. Pick 3 recurring jobs.

    Not dreams, not someday-projects. Things you do every week that you hate doing. The weekly briefing, the expense review, the research you keep putting off.

  2. Write standing instructions once.

    How you want it done. Your rules, your boundaries, your preferences. Be specific — "short emails, plain text, no fluff" beats "write well" every time.

  3. Give it the tools.

    Calendar, reminders, your accounts, your files. An operator without tools is just a chatbot with confidence. The standing instructions tell it what to do; the tools let it actually do it.

The rule that makes it work

It does the work. I make the decisions.

Money moves, claims filed, purchases, anything outward-facing — nothing happens without my explicit yes. That's the line. An operator with judgment about its own limits is useful; one without them is a liability. Set the boundary on day one and it holds.

"On a jobsite, you don't stand over a good hand and narrate every move. You give them the scope, the standards, and the authority — then you check the work. AI is no different."

The superintendent's take

On a jobsite, you don't stand over a good hand and narrate every move. You give them the scope, the standards, and the authority — then you check the work. AI is no different. Most people are micromanaging a search bar. Give it a job instead.
